Supplementary methods – Slice analysis for fractal dimension measurement
To ensure anatomical consistency across all specimens, all four µCT reconstructions encompassing the cats’ heads were re-oriented along the same rotational axes in 3D space. To establish roll alignment, the two lowest points of the mandibles were aligned (Southerden et al., 2018). Pitch was aligned parallel to the palatal plate with a 25 mm ruler positioned along the anterior palate, and the dataset rotated until this reference axis lay parallel to the x-axis in 2D view (Weiss et al., 2004). Yaw was aligned relative to the internarial septum, relying on midline symmetry (Díaz Martínez et al., 2024).

The segmentation approach was tailored to address variations in tissue intensity across the sample even though all specimens were scanned at similar resolutions. The mummified cat RM 2728.1 had nasal turbinate bone intensities similar to the surrounding skull bone. In contrast, the turbinates of the three modern cats had intensities more similar to the image background, which indicated that they were structurally less dense. Consequently, the mummified cat’s nasal turbinates were segmented using the same custom neural network previously trained on the entire skull, in conjunction with a 3D object mask which isolated the nasal cavity from the rest of the skull. No manual segmentations of additional reference slices were required, and surrounding cranial bone volume was manually removed using 2D and 3D ROI tools. In contrast, a new custom neural network was designed and trained to segment the three modern cats’ nasal turbinates (Table S1). This network was trained on manually segmented reference slices from all three modern datasets, primarily along the dorsoventral axis. The African cat RM7303 yielded the poorest initial segmentation results, so additional training rounds which included reference slices along the rostrocaudal axis were required to improve the network’s predictions. Total manually segmented cat reference standards constituted ~ 20% of each 3D volume. A new multi-ROI was generated for each specimen, from which the turbinate class was extracted as a single ROI and manually corrected. Each turbinate ROI was exported as a stack of 8-bit binary TIFF files.

The slice analysis was executed with custom automation scripts. An ImageJ macro was written to iteratively loop through the complete stack of TIFF slices for each specimen. For each individual slice, the image was inverted so that the turbinate ROI corresponded to foreground (counted) pixels, and its space-filling properties were quantified through the calculation of the fractal dimension (D) via the Fractal Box Count plugin, using box sizes of 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 32, and 64 pixels. These sequential D measurements alongside their slice indices were exported as CSV files for each specimen. A custom python script was then deployed for graphical analysis and visualization. To account for interindividual variation in absolute nasal cavity length, the position of each slice was normalized to a percentage scale (0-100%). To smooth the final fractal dimension curves, a centered rolling mean filter was applied. To ensure uniform smoothing across specimens with variable skull lengths, the sliding window was proportionally scaled to 45% of each dataset's total slice count, thus calculating the local arithmetic mean for each slice and its adjacent neighbors. The code and data required to reproduce this slice analysis are available as supplementary material in this repository.

Supplemental discussion – the life of a wild African cat… in captivity
RM 7303 may be a wild cat species (as opposed to the domestic cat Felis catus), but this individual likely spent its later years in captivity. This is because it was declawed following a practice called onychectomy, it bore even healthier teeth than the two domestic cats, and its soft-tissue outline suggests it was better fed than it could ever be in the wild (figure 1G). Moreover, a life in captivity for this individual also explains its only developmental character state to suggest a juvenile stage, i.e., its open growth plates in the femur. These actually represent epiphyseal scars, i.e., vestiges of the growth plates, which hint at a relative lack of physical activity in which bone growth is insufficiently stimulated (Davies et al., 2014, 2015). Since these are not ontogenetically informative, they are not accounted for in the age ranges proposed for the ‘African’ cat.
